# Digital Labor Rights Manifesto

## I.

For most of the twentieth century, workers shared physical space with those who depended on the same wages, answered to the same foreman, and walked out the same factory gate. Organizing happened because you could not avoid the people your employer was also exploiting.

Corporations understood this. Decades of subcontracting, outsourcing, and fragmentation were attempts to thin out that density. Then globalisation and the internet finished the job.

A team of fifty people doing identical work might now be spread across twelve countries, in different time zones, communicating through channels the company controls. They may never speak. They may not even know the others exist.

When one stops getting paid, they assume it's their problem. Most of the time it is not. But there is no infrastructure to let them find out.

The platforms built on top of this model did not create that isolation. They found it ready-made and formalised it. Independent contractor status. Mandatory arbitration clauses that make collective legal action impossible. Terms of service that waive the right to public dispute. Communication routed through company channels, where it can be monitored, deleted, or simply ignored.

The factory floor gave workers one thing the platform economy deliberately withholds: the ability to look sideways and see who else was being treated the same way.

## II.

The platform economy replicates an identical structure everywhere:

- Workers classified as independent contractors, not employees
- Global workforce deliberately sourced from lower-currency countries
- Payment processed through platforms the company controls unilaterally
- Dispute resolution controlled entirely by the company
- Terms of service written exclusively to protect the platform
- Zero collective bargaining infrastructure for workers

This architecture is not accidental. It is a deliberate design for extracting labour while avoiding the legal obligations of employment. Workers in lower-currency countries are specifically targeted because they are less likely to know their legal options, less likely to afford individual legal action, and more economically desperate -- making resistance costly and silence rational.

Individual complaints get ignored. Isolated workers have no leverage. Lawyers will not touch individual small claims. Social media pressure is fleeting. One person shouting into the void changes nothing.

Together, our voices become impossible to silence. This is the collective action problem that Sindicato was built to solve.

## III.

Sindicato is a digital labour rights platform. Workers self-publish their cases -- their own words, their own evidence, their own attestation. The platform aggregates individual reports into collective dashboards per company, quantifying the total number of affected workers, unpaid hours, and monetary debt owed.

It operates as a modern worker syndicate:

- Workers submit their case with structured evidence
- Cases appear publicly on the Cases Wall -- name partially redacted, story fully visible
- Numbers aggregate on the company dashboard
- Automated notifications inform the company each time a new case is filed
- Anonymous aliases enable contact without exposing identity
- Collective totals grow, pressure compounds

The platform never asserts, verifies, or endorses individual claims. It aggregates and displays what individuals submit under their own attestation.

## IV.

The legal framework exists. What workers lack is the evidentiary infrastructure to use it. Individual complaints are too small for legal action. Isolated grievances disappear into bureaucratic voids.

Sindicato provides the infrastructure that makes isolated grievances visible, collective, and actionable. It transforms scattered individual reports into the pattern-based proof that labour law requires.

One unpaid worker is a statistic. A hundred with matching documentation is a case.

The question is not whether the law is favourable. The question is how many workers will act before the window closes.
